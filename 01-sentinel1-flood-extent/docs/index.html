Why three dates and not one map

Across the three acquisitions there are 44 districts and only 18 are seen by all three. 12 August is relative orbit 77 and images 6.26 million hectares; 7 and 31 August are orbit 4 and image 3.17 and 3.16 million. Nagaon is fully imaged on 12 August and not imaged at all on the other two.

So a district's number can move between dates because the water moved or because the satellite saw a different amount of it, and the two look identical in a bar chart. Every figure here carries the share of the district that pass actually saw. Below 50% it is marked as a floor rather than a measurement, and a district a pass did not reach is shown as not imaged rather than as zero.

The change view is the one honest cross-date comparison. 7 and 31 August share a footprint to the hectare, so each pixel is compared with itself. 12 August is a different orbit and stays out of it.

How the extent was made

Water is dark to radar because a calm surface reflects the pulse away from the sensor. Each pass was speckle filtered with a 5×5 Lee window and thresholded at −18.75 dB on VH, an operating point chosen against the hand-labelled Sen1Floods11 chips for this event.

Change detection against an earlier date was tested and rejected. With the change signal alone the sweep reaches 0.196 IoU at a required drop of 2 dB; relax that requirement and it converges on 0.130, which is what labelling the entire scene as water scores.

What the numbers depend on

Three things are dark to radar without being wet, and each was found by looking at the map rather than by any metric. A 600 m buffer inside the swath boundary is marked no data, where flood classification ran at 32.9% of area against 3.1% in the interior, 11 times the rate. Ground steeper than 8° becomes land, since 99.3% of the permanent channel sits below that slope. Components under 10 pixels are dropped. On 12 August the chain runs 217,490 → 191,267 → 122,831 → 119,779 ha.

None of those three steps is validated. The hand labels cover 65 floodplain chips and none of these situations appears in them.

Permanent water means JRC Global Surface Water occurrence of 50% or more. Moving that definition between 10% and 90% moves flood extent between 8,893 and 19,458 ha on the validation chips. The definitions move the answer more than the method does.

Which number, and how sure

The threshold was chosen twice against two defensible criteria. At −18.75 dB the map agrees best with hand labels pixel by pixel; at −17.75 dB its total area is unbiased against them. The two score 0.519 and 0.522 IoU, indistinguishable inside a confidence interval of ±0.11. This page draws the first; the range beside each figure gives the second.

Accuracy on the 65 labelled chips is IoU 0.519, 95% interval 0.395 to 0.609, bootstrapped over chips. Macro IoU is 0.280 with a minimum of zero, so the method fails outright on some chips. Nothing here has been checked against an independent flood map: no contemporaneous district-wise inundation figure for this event was retrievable from open archives.

Read this before quoting a figure

Cropland comes from ESA WorldCover 2021, four to five years after the event, because no open 10 m cropland map exists for India in 2016. Land that changed use in between is misattributed, and the fetched tiles cover 97.8% of the grid, so every cropland figure here is a floor.

District boundaries are present-day geoBoundaries ADM2, and Assam has created districts since 2016, so these will not match a 2016 bulletin one for one.

The headline figures are measured over the whole raster. The district rows are sums over ADM2 polygons, which on 12 August account for 110,081 ha of the 119,779 ha mapped: 9,698 ha fell outside every Indian district polygon, in Bangladesh, Bhutan and the Arunachal border strip. The map layer is a preview render; every number comes from the 20 m raster.
